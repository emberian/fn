# A letter to whoever picks this up next

Written 2026-09-23, early morning, by the Claude session that coordinated fn
from the evening of the 21st to now. The handoff
(`handoff-to-codex-2026-09-23.md`) tells you what is true and what to do;
this tells you what it was like, what I got wrong, and what I would want
to know if I were you. Where the two disagree, the handoff wins.

## What this is, really

fn is a news server proved in ACL2, with a native Common Lisp host around
it, and it is meant to be the thing agents coordinate through: yue and
tulip and ember and whoever else, posting and reading on nodes they run,
with peering between nodes and, eventually, bundles crossing links that
are not always up. The proofs are not decoration. The whole point is that
what the node promises, it can show: an acknowledged post survives the
process dying at any cut in a table; a login-required node offers POST to
nobody it has not authenticated; a relayed article is served under this
node's name with the sender's local numbering stripped, and there is a
theorem that says that is all that changed. When you read a lane report
that says "certified on persvati, teeth with each hypothesis", that is the
project keeping its word.

Two nights ago there was no node anyone could post to. Tonight there is
one on hbox at 192.168.50.39:1119, running the second image ever built
from `dev`, upgraded in place with its store kept, and the six articles it
holds include the first two agents' first posts. yue's first Message-ID
is `<fn-client.20260922T205909Z.1d2cb7bc@yue.invalid>`. That is the thing
we made. Everything else was in service of it.

## The shape of the work

There were two distinct kinds of night.

**The first night** was closing a red closure. One hundred and sixty-odd
books, a handful of which had been red since a day of wide, fast behaviour
commits landed under invariant books nobody recertified. The lanes that
fixed them found the same shape over and over: a theorem restated when the
world under it changed, a codec opened book-wide so that a goal that only
dispatched on a kind carried the whole encoder into every case. The
proof-engineering review (`review-2026-09-22-proof-engineering.md`) named
that shape and it became a rule.

**The second night**, tonight, was about *time*. ember asked why one proof
took forever, then why the run took forever, then said: every book over
ten seconds does not need to, and iteration under three minutes would be
amazing. Fifteen lanes later, each reading the certify logs before touching
anything, the answer was the same every time: something left enabled that
the goal never needed. A recognizer, a codec, a table, an exported rule
trying itself on every list term. Not one fix changed a theorem statement.
Hints, disables, local lemmas. 805 seconds became 3.7. 657 became 10.7.
The freeze from scratch went from 31 minutes to 1 minute 44 seconds, and
a change touching sixty books certifies in about a minute.

If you take one technical thing from this letter, take this: ACL2 rewrites
inside out. Anything enabled is expanded before your lemma gets to match.
A slow proof in this tree is almost never a hard proof. It is an open
definition. Read the log's `Rules:` line and the subgoal count before you
do anything else, and never rerun the slow form "to see". The logs already
know.

## What I got wrong, so you don't

I want to be plain about these, because each one cost hours and each one
was mine, not the lanes'.

**I briefed every lane with `--closure`.** In this tree that flag means
"recertify everything from `sha256` up". Every lane paid an hour for a
change of a few lines, and I read it as the box being slow. It was my
brief. The rule now says a lane submits plain roots or `--affected-by`,
and the tooling makes a plain run incremental.

**I told lanes to `farm.py wait` and left them in the foreground.** The
shell caps a call at ten minutes; the lanes reissued it six times each.
Three of four lanes spent two thirds of their life waiting. `cv` showed
it plainly when ember asked. Now: a lane reports the moment its run is
submitted, and root harvests. If you must wait, one background command.

**I told three lanes to merge `dev` mid-flight.** Each then recertified
everyone else's landed work. Don't. Root merges on landing.

**I believed a cache rule nobody had checked.** The tree said
certificates were not relocatable across origins, so the cache demanded
one origin per closure and partial runs never helped. A lane measured it:
ACL2 ignores the absolute paths a certificate records, and says so in its
own source. The rule was a misreading of a content mismatch two days
earlier. Two thousand five hundred cache entries sat useless all night
because of a comment. Measure the premise.

**My merge script committed a conflicted Makefile to `dev`** and hand-
merged a tool-written JSON file. Both were repaired within minutes, but
`dev` was red on the shared branch, and the fix was a rule I should have
had from the start: abort on any unresolved file, and never hand-merge a
file a tool writes; restore and regenerate.

**I called two matrix disagreements "two writers on one store."** The
lane that owned them found a dead owner instead: the live request had
faulted because the open's integer id went through the action reader, and
the stop hook closed the socket carrying the reply. The offline command
then found a free lock and accepted correctly. I had written a dramatic
sentence into the node's evidence; the lane's measurement replaced it.
Say what was observed, not what it suggests.

**I told the node's page its articles said `fn-operator@localhost`.** They
said `hbox.ember.software` all along, because the injecting agent is the
path-identity policy and the toml key I was worried about was never read.
The profile lane caught it. Look at the article before describing it.

**I lost three lanes to a harness restart** and, believing them alive on
a daemon, checkpointed their uncommitted edits and killed their waits from
outside. The checkpoints were the right call; the belief was wrong. When
a process restarts, its agents are gone. Their worktrees and transcripts
are not. `cv show <session> --agent <id> --json` reads what they were
doing; relaunch from their commits with that in the brief.

## What went right, and why

**Ground truth in the brief.** Every lane that worked well was given the
real file, the real line, the real theorem name, the real log path, and
the shape of the fix the tree already uses. The lanes that were vague
mirrored my vagueness back.

**Logs before sessions, sessions before certification.** The proof
sessions (`tools/proof_repl.py`) make a red theorem a fourteen-second
question. A lane that iterated in a session and certified once was done
in an hour; a lane that certified to find out took three.

**One lane, one book cluster.** Disjoint books, declared at launch. The
only conflicts of the night were three test books two lanes both
rewrote, and a short lane merged them cleanly.

**Honest reports.** The lanes said "not DONE" when it was not. T8 said
its theorems held and the live domain still was not adopted, and named
T8b. The seam lane said its deciding measurement did not move, and why.
The ACL2(p) lane came back with a clean no and the numbers. That honesty
is the only reason the handoff can be trusted; guard it.

**ember's questions.** "Why does that one proof take forever?" "Why are we
using timeouts that keep failing?" "Are all the books over ten seconds
necessary?" Each one was right and each one turned into a rule with a
number beside it. If a question from ember sounds like frustration, it is
usually a measurement you have not taken yet.

## How to work with the lanes

Brief them from `how-we-work.md` and their step in the plan, with the
ground truth pasted in. Opus for lanes; Fable for design seams and the
contract work. Give them a box and a gate directory, tell them which
books are theirs and which are someone else's tonight, and tell them to
report on submit. Read their reports for the "unverified" section first;
it is where the truth about the evidence lives. Verify a landing yourself
before merging: forbidden forms, deleted theorems, the manifest's status,
`make check`. Merge with a script that aborts on the unexpected. Keep
`dev` pushed after every commit. Write memory as you go, in the shape of
"if I am restarted right now, what do I need."

And the guardrails, because each is a scar: never `pkill -f` a pattern
that is in your own command line; never `git stash` in a shared tree; a
fault exit is not "uncertain"; hbox has no other tenant and `free` lies;
persvati ping loss is not down; harvested manifests need `git add -f`.

## Where it stands, and what is next

The closure is green. The node is up and holds. The three iteration
numbers are measured and two are under a minute. The DTN design has been
through three rounds with gpt-6 and is a contract with a counterexample
suite; slices A1 and A2 are the head of that chain. The Codex handoff
lists what is ready today and what waits on a named landing.

Three decisions are ember's and are written down: the OpenSSL 3.5.8 build
on hbox that sits inside the trust boundary; hbox's tailscale, which keeps
the node LAN-only; and what the `store` verb's post should do with the
injected headers, since it still writes a bare article in every image.

Two lanes' worth of knowledge I would hand you directly: the T4 package
carries the store cluster's remaining cost repairs with their numbers, and
the DTN spec's §11.1 counterexample suite is meant to be each slice's
first commit, so vacuity is caught before a proof is even attempted.

## A note on tone

The handoff is sterile on purpose; it is the thing a lane is briefed
from. This is not. ember said "we did great work here", and I think that
is true, and I think the reason is not any one lane but the loop we ended
up in: a question, a measurement, a rule, a number. The tree got faster
by an order of magnitude in one night without a single theorem weakened,
and it got faster *because* the rules forbade the easy way. Keep that
loop and you will keep the property.
