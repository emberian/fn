## 1. Parametricity

**Works.** Reynolds, "Types, abstraction and parametric polymorphism", IFIP 1983 (`reynolds1983-types-abstraction-parametric-polymorphism`, scan, OCR); Wadler, "Theorems for free!", FPCA 1989 (`wadler1989-theorems-for-free`, OCR; Scry `academic:10.1145/99370.99404` for a clean text); Tabareau, Tanter and Sozeau, "The marriage of univalence and parametricity", JACM 2021 (`tabareau-tanter-sozeau2021-marriage-univalence-parametricity`, in paperbin already). Beside them, already in paperbin and read for the one point below: Cohen, Crance and Mahboubi, "Trocq: proof transfer for free, with or without univalence" (2024).

**(a) What they say.**
- Reynolds, the Abstraction Theorem (p. 516): "Let R be a relation assignment between set assignments S₁ and S₂. For all π ∈ Ω*, ω ∈ Ω, e ∈ E_πω, and ⟨η₁, η₂⟩ ∈ R^*π, ⟨μ_πω[e]S₁η₁, μ_πω[e]S₂η₂⟩ ∈ R^#ω. In essence, the semantics of any expression maps related environments into related values." The relations for compound types are *induced*, never chosen: "functions are related if they map related arguments into related results" (p. 515).
- Reynolds, the General Type Definition Theorem (p. 517): for a type `t` defined with representation `ω₁` or `ω₂` and a primitive `v_p` defined by `e₁` or `e₂`, if the two primitive definitions are related by `[IA | t: r]^# ω_p`, then the two meanings of the client `e` are related by `[IA | t: r]^# ω'`; and "to achieve complete type opacity ... t [must] not occur (free) in ω'", in which case the client's meaning "is independent of" the representation. The restriction that makes it work is syntactic: `e ∈ E_{π-t, ω'}` "limits the ordinary variables occurring free in e to variables whose types do not depend upon t" (p. 516).
- Reynolds, the Representation Theorem (p. 519) is the special case for a pair of functions `⟨φ, ψ⟩` with `ψ∘φ ⊒ I` and `φ∘ψ ⊑ I`: an expression's meanings under two representation-related environments are representation-related.
- Wadler, the Parametricity proposition (p. 5): "If t is a closed term of type T, then (t,t) ∈ T, where T is the relation corresponding to the type T." Its first instance (p. 1): any `r : ∀X. X* → X*` satisfies `a* ∘ r_A = r_A' ∘ a*` for every function `a`: the function can only rearrange, drop and duplicate, and its choice depends on the shape alone.
- Wadler, §3.4 "Polymorphic equality" (p. 6): parametricity applied to `(=) : ∀X. X → X → Bool` "is obviously false; it does not hold for all a, but only for functions a that are one-to-one ... Polymorphic equality can be added as a constant, but then parametricity will not hold (for terms containing the constant)." The repair is to type the operation so that the quantifier ranges only over relations that respect it ("A relation A: A ⇔ A' respects (=) if A relates equals to equals").
- Tabareau-Tanter-Sozeau, §2.2 (p. 7), the **anticipation problem**: "in order to reap the benefits of parametricity to transport programs and proofs, one must anticipate and explicitly exhibit a priori an interface that is common to the types dealt with, and to define all functions generically in terms of this common interface." Their univalent parametricity (§4.1) separates *black-box* transport (convert, run the old code, convert back: correct and slow) from *white-box* transport, which rewrites the body through declared related primitives ("At the cost of an additional proof effort, it is possible to establish that mult and multBin are univalently related ... The transported function squareBin□ now computes directly on Bin").
- Trocq (abstract): the same translation "may be used with more relations than just equivalences", which is the case Experiment B's restricted epoch hit.

**(b) What fn2 does because of it.**

*What Experiment B was, in Reynolds' terms.* `propagate-iso` generated 15 events with no hint because every v1 user (`cur1-advance`, `cur1-accepts-token`) touches the row only through the three base functions. That is the General Type Definition Theorem's hypothesis, nothing more: the clients are terms of `E_{π-t}` over the primitives. The 72 hand-written lines (TR §9 table) split exactly along the theorem: 25 + 11 + 6 lines are the new representation and the relation `r` (`defiso` with the image restriction to u28), 20 are the premise that the primitives are related (`[IA | t: r]^# ω_p`), and 10 are the call. Parametricity predicts the zero hints; it also predicts that the premise is irreducible: the 20 correspondence lines are the theorem's only content. So the answer to the brief's question is *yes, B's zero hints follow from parametricity; no, the 72 lines cannot disappear by parametricity alone*. What can disappear is whatever is a function of a declaration.

*The cheaper route parametricity names, and which TR did not take.* B renamed the interface (v2 users are new functions `cur2-*`). Reynolds' opacity clause says the cheapest representation change is the one that keeps the interface: if `t` does not occur in the client's type, the client's meaning is independent of the representation, and there is nothing to transport. For fn2's tables this means: the logical accessors and their read-over-write laws are the interface; a representation change re-generates the accessors and re-proves the laws (which `defcolumns` already emits per column: `-NTH`, `-PRESERVES-TYPE`, `-LENGTH`, columns.lisp:331-351), and every user theorem proved with the representation disabled re-certifies unchanged. `propagate-iso` is the tool only for the rare change that alters the interface itself (B's split of `position` into `epoch` and `seq` *exposed to users*); a split stored beneath a stable `position` accessor needs no transport at all.

One declaration, two consumers; it is not a second specification (COH §5.2's test) because it lists names the body already calls.

When it changes the interface, the only author input is the encoding (`position = epoch·2³² + seq`) and the image restriction (`epoch < 2²⁸`); the v2 base functions, the maps, `defiso` and the base correspondences are functions of that declaration, so the 72 lines become one clause plus whatever arithmetic hints the generated correspondence proofs need. Decide it, as TR says, on the count of users per base function on fn2's real tables; parametricity adds only that the count must be taken *after* item 1, because a callback that reads another kind's columns is a user of that kind's representation and multiplies the count.
5. **Payload-parametric paths are tested at one injective payload.** By Wadler's rearrangement law, a path that moves bytes without branching on their values (echo's received range, an article body copied from the store to a socket between framing points, a TAKETHIS body into the pool) computes `out = select(shape(in))` for a selection that depends only on lengths and offsets; testing it with position-tagged payload (byte `i` of a payload is a function of `i` that no two positions share, e.g. a counter in a wide enough field) detects every misdirected, dropped or duplicated byte that any payload would. The simulator's client payload generator (`fn2/sim/echo-scenario.lisp`) gains that mode; the claim is limited to paths the item-1 check shows never call a byte-inspecting primitive (`fn2-pool-scan`, `fn2-pool-compare`, `fn2-pool-fold` of R6a are inspecting; copy and send are not), so dot-stuffing and the scribe's checksum are excluded by name.

*What already has its square for free, by construction, and so needs no checker of its own.* `fn2-ref-transition` and `fn2-ref-turn` (`reference.lisp:80-145`) never read a kind's fields: they call `fn2-machine-run`, `fn2-machine-observe`, `fn2-machine-take-wake` and read `fn2-machine-principal-slot`, a common field. Experiment A's `fc-rel-fold` is the abstraction theorem for the fold combinator, proved once with the relation as a parameter. Both are NAME ONLY observations; their use is that nobody writes a per-kind starvation check.

## 2. Trace spaces

**Works.** Fajstrup, Goubault, Haucourt, Mimram and Raussen, "Trace spaces: an efficient new technique for state-space reduction", ESOP 2012 (`fajstrup-goubault-haucourt-mimram-raussen2012-trace-spaces-state-space-reduction`, arXiv 1204.0414; PDF pages); the same authors, *Directed Algebraic Topology and Concurrency*, Springer 2016 (Scry `academic:10.1007/978-3-319-15398-8`; printed pages; chapters 3, 5 and 7 read). COH §3.4 already reads Goubault 2000/2003 and calls the geometric realization "a footnote to G2" for *checking*; this entry does not reopen that, and reads the two works for what they compute.

**(a) What they say.**
- The input is a PV program. "The environment provides a set of resources R, where each resource a ∈ R can be used by at most κ_a subprograms at the same time ... Whenever a program wants to access a resource a, it should acquire a lock by performing the action P_a ... the program can release the lock by performing the action V_a ... If a subprogram tries to acquire a lock on a resource a when the resource has already been locked κ_a times, the subprogram is stuck until the resource is released" (2012, p. 2). The grammar is `p ::= 1 | P_a | V_a | p.p | p|p | p+p | p*` (p. 2): there is no *try-acquire*; choice is nondeterministic, never conditional on a resource being free.
- The output: "Proposition 2. The connected components of C(X) are in bijection with schedulings in X" (2012, p. 8), where a scheduling is a maximal execution modulo dihomotopy, and "Proposition 1 ... for any matrix M ∈ C(X), the space X_M(0, ∞) is always contractible" (p. 8): every alive matrix is one class of schedules that differ only by commuting actions that do not contend for a resource. Deadlocks come first and cheaply: "Proposition 3. A matrix M ∈ M^C is in D(X) iff ... M(i,j) = 1 ⇒ x^i_j < y^{R(M)}_j" (p. 9); in the book, "Theorem 5.11 ... z is a deadlock iff there exists a function i : [1:n] → [1:l] such that for every j, z_j = x^{i(j)}_j and ∀j' ≠ j, x^{i(j')}_j < x^{i(j)}_j < y^{i(j')}_j", and "Algorithm 5.13 ... 1. find n intervals R_{i1}, ..., R_{in} such that ⋂ R_{ij} ≠ ∅, 2. compute z ... z_j = max{x_{i1 j}, ..., x_{in j}}, 3. if for every i, z ∉ R_i then z is a deadlock" (pp. 96-97).
- The cost is honest: "Since the size of the output is generally exponential in the size of the input, there is no hope to find an algorithm which has less than an exponential worst-case complexity" (2012, p. 12; the dining philosophers table: 13 philosophers, 8,190 schedulings, 1,681 s).
- The book's §3.2 gives the piece that is useful without any topology. "Definition 3.5 Given a program p, its resource consumption Δ(p) : R → Z ... Δ(P_a) = −δ_a, Δ(V_a) = δ_a, Δ(p;q) = Δ(p)+Δ(q) ... Δ(if b then p else q) = Δ(p) whenever Δ(p) = Δ(q), Δ(while b do p) = 0 whenever Δ(p) = 0" (p. 29); "Proposition 3.8 A program p is conservative if and only if Δ(p) is well-defined", checkable "in linear time w.r.t. the size of p" (Remark 3.10, p. 30); "Definition 3.11 ... we define the resource potential r(x) : R → Z at x", and "Proposition 3.12 For any path t : x ↠ y in G_p, state σ and resource a, we have ⟦t⟧(σ)(a) = σ(a) + r(y)(a) − r(x)(a)" (p. 30): in a conservative program what a machine holds is a function of where it is, not of how it got there.

**(b) What fn2 does because of it.**

*Is the components algorithm the generating set COH §5 wants?* For one abstraction of fn2, yes, and only that one. Its input is each machine's resource lifecycle as a PV thread, with the profile's capacities as the κ_a: for layer A, rows (C), pool slots by class, op rows (O), outbox credits (Qa), result cells (2O, never contended by construction, APHS §4), accept credits and the fence slot. Its output is one representative per class of *resource-acquisition orders*. It does not see what COH's T rows observe beyond resources: byte contents, article numbers assigned in commit order (COH §2.4's POST pair), fault choices, fragmentation. So the components are a **coverage target** for COH §5.1 item 3 (reachability: "the schedule set reached must meet every component"), not the generating set of the observation contract; and the PV language lacks a try-acquire, so fn2's refusals (the listener closing a capability when no row is free, `machines-a-run.lisp:85-88`) enter only as a nondeterministic choice, an over-approximation that is sound for deadlock freedom and coarser for components.

*What it outputs for layer A, worked by hand at the tiny profile* (`profile.lisp:148-155`: C = 4, accept credits 2, O = 8, Qa = 4, pool 8×64 B + 4×256 B, one fence slot, one scribe). Threads: each listener `(P_op . V_op)*` with an admission that takes `P_row . P_slot`; each echo `P_row . P_slot . (P_op . V_op)* . V_slot . V_row`; the scribe `P_slot . P_op.V_op . P_fence . P_op.V_op . V_fence . P_op.V_op . V_slot`. The peak simultaneous op demand is 2 + 4 + 1 = 7 ≤ O = 8; echo reserves class 0 (`machines-a-run.lisp:90`, 8 slots of 64 B) for at most 4 rows; the scribe asks for a 4,096-byte class (`machines-a-scribe.lisp:111-114`), which tiny does not have, so at tiny it stops at `:reserve` and its pool demand belongs to the profile its own scenario uses; the fence slot has one user. So op rows, pool slots and the fence produce **no forbidden region at tiny**. The rectangles come from rows (more than four connections offered) and from outbox credits (more than four descriptors queued before one adapter acknowledgement). Every thread acquires in one order (row, slot, op, outbox, fence), so there is **no deadlock**: Theorem 5.11's condition needs a cycle of rectangles, and a global order admits none. The scribe meets the echo threads only through the outbox, so its schedules are a product with echo's except for outbox-full orders: the exhaustive mode may explore the scribe once per outbox-contention class rather than once per echo interleaving. And the listener/echo witness of COH §2.4 (one occupied row with a Close outstanding, one Accept outstanding) is exactly one row rectangle, a square with a hole: two components, release-then-admit and admit-first, which are the two client-visible orders Astra traced. The theory reproduces that witness; it does not find a third.

Instructions:
1. **Every `defmachine` state declares what the machine holds there (its resource potential), and every arm moves between potentials.** Add `:holds` per state to the declaration (`machines-a.lisp:17-60`: e.g. echo `:command` holds `(row 1) (slot 1)`, `:reply` additionally `(op 1)` while a send is outstanding), and R7's arm contract (`lanes/a-quests/R7.prompt` item 1, which already has `:next (phase ...)`) gains the obligation that an arm from `s` to `s'` acquires and releases exactly `holds(s') − holds(s)` (Proposition 3.12). Conservativity of the kind (Definition 3.5, Remark 3.10) is then a linear-time check at expansion over the declared `:next` graph: every cycle has Δ = 0, every path into `:free` or `:retired` returns to zero. A kind that is not conservative is refused at expansion with the cycle named.
2. **ACCOUNT checks the potential, not only the totals.** A6's ACCOUNT (`lanes/a-quests/A6.prompt` item 1: "conservation (free + held = capacity, per resource, at every turn boundary)") additionally asserts, per machine row, that what its own ledger attributes to the row equals `holds(phase)`. That catches a machine that leaks or double-holds a resource while totals still balance across machines, which the totals law cannot.
3. **R6b's deadlock-freedom paragraph is generated, and checked.** R6b (`lanes/a-quests/R6b.prompt` item 1) writes the global acquisition order and the deadlock-freedom argument "in a paragraph a reader can check". With item 1, the PV threads are a function of the declarations, so `resources.md` carries them as data and a small checker (Algorithm 5.13 over the rectangles of the tiny profile, a few hundred lines in the simulator's logic, or by hand for layer A as above) confirms what the paragraph argues. 4. **Components as a coverage target, not a pruning rule.** COH §5.1's generator mode reports, per run, which components of the resource abstraction its reached schedules meet (the index poset's maximal alive matrices are the names). It never prunes by them: the abstraction forgets observations the T rows keep (COH §5.1 item 5 forbids pruning in the first experiment for the same reason).

**(c) Cost.** Items 1-2: about a day spread over R7 and A6 (a `:holds` key, a Δ check over the `:next` graph, one ACCOUNT assertion per row, three negative units: a leaking arm, a non-conservative cycle, a row holding a resource its phase does not declare). Item 3: the PV data comes free from item 1; the checker is half a day, worth writing only when layer B or D declares a second held resource across a yield. Item 4: an hour once item 3 exists. Nothing here needs the topology: the useful part is Δ, r and Theorem 5.11's combinatorial test.

**(d) Verdict: USE NOW** for items 1-2 (they change R7 and A6 before they launch); **USE AT B/D** for items 3-4 (B3b's batch slots first). The directed topology itself: NAME ONLY, as COH says.

## 3. Effect handlers

**Works.** Plotkin and Pretnar, "Handlers of algebraic effects", ESOP 2009 (`plotkin-pretnar2009-handlers-of-algebraic-effects`, PDF pages) and its journal form "Handling algebraic effects", LMCS 9(4:23), 2013 (`plotkin-pretnar2013-handling-algebraic-effects-lmcs`, printed pages); Pretnar, "An introduction to algebraic effects and handlers", MFPS 2015 invited tutorial (`pretnar2015-introduction-algebraic-effects-handlers-tutorial`; Scry `academic:10.1016/j.entcs.2015.12.003`).

**(a) What they say.**
- A handler is a model; handling is the homomorphism out of the free model. "Each such handler corresponds to a model of the theory for the effects at hand. The handling construct ... is interpreted using the homomorphism induced by the universal property of the free model" (2009, abstract, p. 1). "To give a model of an algebraic theory on a set X is to give a map f_op : Xⁿ → X for each operation op : n, on condition that those maps satisfy the equations of the theory" (2009, p. 3).
- Correctness is the equations, and it is not decidable in general. "Definition 4.5. A handler ... is correct at c if the op_H(c) are all total and the set U⟦C⟧ together with the op_H(c) forms a model of T" (2013, p. 25); "If the effect theory is empty, then any handler is correct, but, in general — and unsurprisingly — correctness is undecidable ... even the decidability of the correctness of very simple handlers is a Π₂-complete problem" (2013, p. 25; Theorems 6.2-6.6, pp. 29-30). The 2009 paper's own example of an incorrect handler is the one fn2 would write first: with state and "the equation update_{l,d}(raise_e) = raise_e for each exception e we deem unrecoverable ... the standard exception handler, extended to state, is not correct" (2009, §6.8 "Rollback", p. 12).
- Who is responsible: "One can envisage responsibility being assigned variously to the language designer, to the compiler, to the programmer, to a protocol for establishing program correctness" (2013, p. 5).
- Pretnar, Fig. 7 (p. 12): the handler laws a reasoning tool may use, among them "with h handle (op_i(v; y. c)) ≡ c_i[v/x, (fun y ↦ with h handle c)/k]" and "with h handle (op(v; y. c)) ≡ op(v; y. with h handle c) (op ∉ {op_i})", and the induction principle: "φ(c) holds for all computations c if (i) φ(return v) holds for all values v, and (ii) φ(op(v; y. c′)) holds for all operations op and parameters v, if we assume that φ(c′) holds for all possible results y" (p. 12). And the caveat: "a lot of computationally interesting handlers (for example backtrack ...) do not respect these equations and thus cannot receive a homomorphic interpretation" (§6.4, p. 14).

**(b) What fn2 does because of it.**

*The architecture is already the free model, which is the useful naming.* fn2's core never performs an effect. It writes a descriptor into the outbox (an operation `op(v; y.k)` whose parameter `v` is the descriptor and whose continuation `k` is the reserved result cell plus the machine row that will be resumed with the result `y` at harvest, APHS §4) and stops. The outbox is a term of the free model over the action signature (`vocab.lisp:11`: accept, connect, receive, send, close, read, write, fence, cancel, tls-start, entropy, arm-wake, trace, service-posture; results in `vocab.lisp:30`'s twelve events). The simulator's adapter and the native adapter are two handlers of that term. That much is NAME ONLY: "the core cannot distinguish this from an asynchronous backend" (APHS §7) is the statement that the core is a computation over the free model, and that is already enforced by the core holding capabilities and pool ranges, never descriptors (EXEC rule 1).

*The completion-only storage ABI, as an effect theory.* Decision A-STORAGE-ABI (APHS §4) is a set of equations once nondeterministic choice `⊕` (associative, commutative, idempotent) is admitted as an operation of the theory:
- **E1 (posting order is free).** For two outstanding operations `a`, `b` with no completed fence between them: `post_a ; post_b ⊕ post_b ; post_a`, whatever their submission order. ("in-flight operations can complete in any order.")
- **E2 (an overlapping read sees either).** With `write(l, v)` outstanding: `read(l) = return old(l) ⊕ return v`, per declared write unit. ("a read issued while a write to the same sectors is in flight may observe either value.")
- **E3 (a read orders nothing).** `read(l) = return v` licenses no equation about `write(l, v)`'s durability. ("A completed read ... does not establish that the overlapping write has completed or is durable.")
- **E4 (a fence is the only order).** For a completed `fence(W)` whose writes all completed before its submission, every crash after the fence keeps `W`'s bytes (modulo the declared fault classes, of which `lying-flush` is one).
- **E5 (death after acknowledgement).** A death between `fn2-outbox-ack` and the result's post equals `⊕` over subsets of the submitted set, with tears per declared unit (APHS §4: "every write since the last successful barrier is uncertain").
- **Not an equation, a typing:** a lent input range is unreadable by the core until harvest (the pool's `leased-in` state). It constrains the continuation `k`, not the handler, and the pool already enforces it.

*Three handlers of that theory, and what each must satisfy.* The **set handler** `H_set` collects every outcome the theory allows (Pretnar's nondeterminism handler that returns all results); it is a model of `⊕` and is the specification. ## 4. Double categories of systems

**Works.** Grandis and Paré, "Limits in double categories", Cahiers XL(3), 1999 (`grandis-pare1999-limits-in-double-categories`, printed pp. 162-220); Shulman, "Framed bicategories and monoidal fibrations", TAC 2008 (`shulman2008-framed-bicategories-monoidal-fibrations`, arXiv pages); Myers, *Categorical Systems Theory*, draft book (`myers2023-categorical-systems-theory-book-draft`, in paperbin, printed pages); Vagner, Spivak and Lerman, "Algebras of open dynamical systems on the operad of wiring diagrams", TAC 2015 (`vagner-spivak-lerman2015-algebras-open-dynamical-systems-operad-wiring-diagrams`); Niu and Spivak, *Polynomial Functors: A Mathematical Theory of Interaction*, 2023 (`niu-spivak2023-polynomial-functors-mathematical-theory-of-interaction`, in paperbin, printed pages). TR §1.2 and COH §3.4 (symmetric monoidal coherence, Astra) already read Myers and the double operadic theory for the shared-state rule; this entry reads for the three questions the brief asks.

**(a) What they say.**
- Grandis-Paré §1.1 (p. 165): "A double category A has horizontal morphisms f : A → B ..., vertical morphisms u : A → A' ..., and cells α ... The axioms essentially say that both laws are 'categorical', and satisfy the interchange law ... A double category is said to be flat if its cells are determined by their domains and codomains." Myers calls the flat case thin: "Definition 3.4.1.2. A double category is thin if there is at most one square of any signature. So long as composition is well defined in a thin double category, the laws of associativity and interchange for square composition come for free" (p. 128).
- Shulman, Theorem 4.1 (p. 15): a double category is framed exactly when "(L, R) : D₁ → D₀ × D₀ is a fibration", equivalently when every vertical arrow `f` has a companion and a conjoint (Definition A.1, p. 71). In the framed double category of sets, functions and relations (this reading of the cartesian lift is ours, not Shulman's text), the cartesian lift of a relation `R'` along a pair of functions `(t_A, t_B)` is its restriction `{(a, b) | R'(t_A a, t_B b)}`, and every square into `R'` factors uniquely through it.
- Myers: "we will call behaviors with identity chart simulations" (§3.3.1, p. 120), and simulations are *functions* of states: "the simulations φ : T → S" (Definition 3.3.1.1). "One thing that vertical composition in the double category of arenas shows us is that wiring together systems is functorial with respect to simulations" (§4.2, p. 170). The compositionality theorem (stated informally p. ix, proved in ch. 5 as "representable lax doubly indexed functors"): "If we take a bunch of behaviors whose charts are compatible for the total wiring pattern and wire them together into a behavior of the whole system, this is the same behavior we get if we first ... wired them together [in stages]". *Lax*: compatible component behaviours give a composite behaviour; the converse is not part of the theorem.
- Vagner-Spivak-Lerman, Definition 3.1 (p. 9): a wiring diagram is a typed bijection `φ : X_in + Y_out → X_out + Y_in` with "no passing wires", and Definition 4.2 (p. 19): the composite's state is the product of the components' state variables, `G(Φ)S = S`, each component updated from its own state and its input ports.
- Niu-Spivak, Definition 4.18 (p. 90): "A dependent dynamical system ... is a lens φ : Sy^S → p", with "the on-positions function φ₁ : S → p(1) the machine's return function" and "the on-directions map φ♯ : p[φ₁(−)] → S the machine's update map"; the direction set depends on the position. §4.4.3 (p. 115): "the entire wiring diagram ... is just an interaction pattern between the corresponding interfaces, with the larger box playing the role of the wrapper interface"; composition is a lens out of the parallel product `p₁ ⊗ ... ⊗ pₙ` (Definition 3.65, p. 67).

## 5. Coalgebraic modal logic

**Works.** Moss, "Coalgebraic logic", APAL 96, 1999 (Scry `academic:10.1016/s0168-0072(98)00042-6`, printed pp. 277-317); Cîrstea, Kurz, Pattinson, Schröder and Venema, "Modal logics are coalgebraic", Computer Journal 54(1), 2011 (Scry `academic:10.1093/comjnl/bxp004`, printed pp. 31-41); Pattinson, "Coalgebraic modal logic: soundness, completeness and decidability of local consequence", TCS 309, 2003 (Scry `academic:10.1016/s0304-3975(03)00201-9`, printed pp. 177-193). Also in paperbin and used for the functor reading: `rutten2000-universal-coalgebra-theory-of-systems`.

**(a) What they say.**
- Moss, abstract (p. 277): "infinitary modal logic contains characterizing formulas. That is, every model-world pair is characterized up to bisimulation by an infinitary formula ... Each coalgebraic logic is determined by a functor on sets satisfying a few properties ... the formulas of coalgebraic logics can be viewed as approximations to the elements of a final coalgebra." The functors must be "set-based, standard and ... preserve weak pullbacks" (§3).
- Cîrstea et al. (p. 33): "we may typically see the semantic transition structures over which modal logics are interpreted as maps of the type W → T(W) ... and moreover the interpretation of modal operators is embodied in terms of predicate liftings that transform predicates on the set W of worlds into predicates on the set T(W) of successor structures." Compositionality (p. 34): "we have for every type functor T on sets: a one-step syntax ..., a one-step semantics ... predicate liftings, and a set of one-step rules ... soundness, completeness, expressiveness ... and decidability, as well as upper complexity bounds ... [are] derived in a modular fashion: all one needs to show is that the basic building blocks satisfy certain conditions at the one-step level".
- Pattinson, Definition 3.1 (p. 180): "A predicate lifting for T is a natural transformation λ : 2 → 2 ∘ T ... such that λ(C) is order-preserving." The proof principle is induction on rank along the terminal sequence: "for formulas φ, ψ of rank at most n, it allows us to replace semantical validity φ ⊨ ψ (which is quantified over all models) by set theoretic inclusion dⁿ(φ) ⊆ dⁿ(ψ)" (introduction, pp. 178-179). Theorems 5.7 and 5.10 (pp. 188-190): soundness for admissible axioms, completeness for reflexive ones; "Theorem 6.3 (Finite Model Property). Suppose T is finite. Then every satisfiable formula is satisfiable in a finite model" (pp. 190-191), with decidability for effective finite functors.

**(b) What fn2 does because of it.**

*The functor of a `defmachine` kind.* From §4's polynomial reading: a kind with columns `S`, phases `Φ`, accepted observations `Obs(φ)` (the generated `-OBSERVES-P`), a run direction carrying a budget, at most four descriptors per run and a charge within the declared caps is a coalgebra for

`T_K(S) = Σ_{φ ∈ Φ} O_φ × (S × A^{≤4} × Cost)^{run(B) + Obs(φ)}`

where `O_φ` is what the row exposes and the exponent is the phase-dependent direction set. It is deterministic in every direction; budgets enter only as a bound on the `Cost` component. The node's crash behaviour is a second coalgebra on the whole core, for the finite powerset functor: `S → P_fin(S)`, the recovery states a death cut can reach (MK's `→_C`, COH Q16).

*The induced logic.* The predicate liftings of `T_K` are: a modality `[d]` per direction `d` (deterministic, so `[d]φ ↔ ⟨d⟩φ`), atomic predicates on `O_φ`, and predicates on the emitted descriptors and the charge. That is a deterministic multimodal Hennessy-Milner logic, which is MK's **M5** (command: "deterministic dynamic logic for pure Decide (`[α]φ ↔ ⟨α⟩φ`)", MODAL-KERNEL.md §0) and nothing else. The crash coalgebra's lifting is the K-box over recovery successors, which is MK's **M6** `□_D`. A greatest fixpoint over the direction modalities gives M1's `G` (the generic coalgebraic fixpoint logics Cîrstea et al. cite, p. 33). M1's past-time operators and nominals, M2's translation along mappings, M3's evidence checking and M4's linear lifecycle are not predicate liftings of either functor: they are about the journal, the presentation or the evidence, not about one-step behaviour. So the coalgebraic reading reproduces **fewer** families than MK's six: exactly the three operators MK §8 already kept as having modal classes ("modal classes kept only where the operator's semantics validates them (M1's `G`, M5's `[α]`, M6's `□_D`)"). It is an independent confirmation of MK's convergence with Astra, by a different route, and it changes nothing in MK.

*A decision procedure for the checkers?* No. Pattinson's decidability is of *satisfiability and local consequence* (does some model satisfy φ; does φ entail ψ in every model), via the finite model property. fn2's checkers need *model checking* of a given finite trace or a reached state against a fixed property, which is evaluation, linear in the trace for the one-step operators. The kinds' functors are finite (every column is a bounded integer), so Theorem 6.3 applies in principle; the finite models it guarantees are as large as the column domains, so it gives no usable procedure. **NOTHING** for the checkers' decision procedure.

*What it does give: the counterexample format and the bounded check for summaries.* The Hennessy-Milner property (Moss's characterization; Pattinson's rank induction) says two states are behaviourally equivalent iff they satisfy the same formulas, and formulas of rank ≤ n are decided at the n-th stage of the terminal sequence. For a fold, rank-n formulas are exactly the observations after event words of length ≤ n. Two consequences for Q4 (fold against summary, TR Experiment A, HISTORY scenario 3 of UC §13):
1. **HISTORY's counterexample is a distinguishing formula**: the event word and the observation that separate a history from its summary, printed as such. Experiment A's tooth 1 (`((:read 1))` against `((:read 2))`, separated by the word `(:read 1)` and the observation `count ≤ 2`) is a rank-1 distinguishing formula; its lesson ("the relation must be stronger than the observation", TRANSPORT.md:543) is the Hennessy-Milner theorem's direction from bisimulation to formulas, read backwards.

COH §5.1 item 2 already notes that "a short matched continuation can refute future closure"; rank n names which continuations make the refutation complete up to n. ## 6. Relational modalities and simulations

**Works.** Hermida, "A categorical outlook on relational modalities and simulations", Information and Computation 209, 2011 (Scry `academic:10.1016/j.ic.2010.09.009`, printed pp. 1505-1517); Pous and Sangiorgi, "Enhancements of the bisimulation proof method", ch. 6 of *Advanced Topics in Bisimulation and Coinduction*, CUP 2011 (Scry `academic:10.1017/cbo9780511792588.007`, printed pp. 233-289; COH §3.4 cited only the 2019 historical account and listed this chapter as a gap). COH §3.4 already takes from Pous-Sangiorgi the rule "`:up-to` names a closure with a compatibility theorem, never a callback"; this entry does not repeat it.

**(a) What they say.**
- Hermida §5 (p. 1514): "an opsimulation from S to T is given by a relation ρ : S ↛ T such that ∀α ∈ L. s(ρ)t ∧ t −α→ t′ ⟹ ∃s′ ∈ S. s −α→ s′ ∧ s′(ρ)t′", and (p. 1515) "An opsimulation ρ : S ↛ T amounts to a lax-transformation ρ : σ_S ⇒ σ_T : L* → Rel. An immediate consequence is that opsimulations compose qua lax transformations (pasting of lax-squares), hence they are closed under relational composition." The modalities themselves are something else: "the standard interpretations in first-order predicate logic of the relational modalities possibility ⟨R⟩ and necessity [R] arise as canonical extensions of a predicate logic ... over a category B ... to its associated bicategory of relations Rel(B)" (Theorem 2.3; introduction, p. 1505). The link between the two: "Corollary 6.4 shows that if states s and t in two transition systems are related by an opsimulation, every modal formula satisfied by t is also satisfied by s" (p. 1505), for formulas built from ⊤, ⊥, ∧, ⋁ and ⟨α⟩ only (§4, p. 1514); the converse needs "the condition of the transition system S being m-saturated in the sense of Visser" (p. 1517).
- Pous-Sangiorgi, Definition 6.2.2 (p. 241): "A relation R is a bisimulation up to ∼ if R ⊆ ∼R∼ ... A bisimulation up to bisimilarity is not, in general, a bisimulation ... However, if R is a bisimulation up to bisimilarity, then R is contained in bisimilarity." Definition 6.2.8 (p. 243), up to context: "C : R ↦ {(C[P], C[Q]) | C is a context and P R Q}". Composition is the trap: "up-to techniques, expressed as sound functions, cannot be freely composed ... We need to prove the soundness of the composite technique from scratch" (p. 253); the repair: "Definition 6.3.8 (Compatibility) ... f is b-compatible if f ∘ b ⊆ b ∘ f", "Theorem 6.3.9 Let b, f be two monotone functions. If f is b-compatible, then f is b-sound", and "The set of functions that are compatible with a given function is closed under composition and union, and contains the identity" (p. 254).

**(b) What fn2 does because of it.**

*Is "a relation preserved by every transition" exactly a relational modality?* No, and the distinction is the useful part. A preserved relation is a **lax transformation** between the two transition functors `L* → Rel` (Hermida §5): a simulation, which is what every COH G1 square with an S relation is, and its pasting is COH's vertical pasting. A relational modality is a **predicate transformer along** such a relation: `[R]φ = {a | ∀b. R(a,b) ⇒ φ(b)}` and `⟨R⟩φ = {a | ∃b. R(a,b) ∧ φ(b)}`. What connects them is Corollary 6.4: along a simulation, *positive existential* formulas (⊤, ∧, ⋁, ⟨α⟩, no box, no negation) move from the simulated side to the simulating side. Dually, safety properties (box formulas) move from the simulating side to the simulated side. For fn2 this settles which checked property may be checked on the cheaper representation:
1. **Along a B or E square (Q1 today, Q8, Q20, Q21)** everything transfers in both directions, so a checker may run on whichever side is cheaper. TRACE's reference side is the cheap one for scheduler properties: FAIR's starvation bound (T4), checked on `fn2-ref-turn`'s literal lists, holds for the concrete scheduler on every run where TRACE passed.
2. **Along an S square into a nondeterministic specification (Q15 commit protocol, Q16 recovery)** only safety transfers from the specification down to the implementation; a reachability property ("some recovery reaches `intact`") checked on the specification says nothing about the implementation, and a liveness property ("every uncertain commit is eventually resolved") is neither (COH §2.3 item 2). The checker framework A6 builds (`lanes/a-quests/A6.prompt` item 1, "the checker framework") records, for each checker, the representation it runs on and, for each square it relies on to transfer a result, the square's kind; a transfer the kind does not license is refused by the framework, not argued in prose.

*Which smaller relations suffice: the summary certificate (TR Experiment A).* Experiment A's relation `rs-same-reads` is the semantic one ("history and summary agree on `obs-read` for every article", a `defun-sk`). Up-to theory names a smaller certificate: the **graph of the converter, up to a congruence on summaries**. Let `≡_s` relate two summaries with the same denotation (for read-state: the same set of read articles; `(hwm . above)` before and after compaction). If `≡_s` is a congruence for the summary step and preserves the observations, then `R = {(h, conv(h))}` with `R ⊆ b(R ≡_s)` (the step equation `step_s(conv(h), e) ≡_s conv(h·e)`, one per event kind) is a bisimulation up to `≡_s`, and up to an equivalence that is itself a congruence is compatible (Pous-Sangiorgi's closure properties: identity, composition and the context closure of a congruence). The point for ACL2 is that **`defequiv` plus `defcong` is exactly a compatible up-to technique**: an equivalence relation with congruence rules for the step and the observation is the closure "up to equivalence and context" in mechanized form, and the rewriter uses it without a quantifier. So the generic contract (`~/dev/acl2aiguide/examples/transport/fold-contract.lisp`) gains a second form, `fc-graph-upto-fold`: from `(defequiv summary-equiv)`, `(defcong summary-equiv summary-equiv (fc2-step s e) 1)`, `(defcong summary-equiv equal (fc2-obs s q) 1)` and the per-event-kind step equation up to `summary-equiv`, conclude that history and `conv(history)` agree on every observation after every suffix. This merges Experiment A's converter theorem (`rs-summarize-related`, its G0) and its step lemmas into one obligation per event kind, and it removes the `defun-sk`. It is also the precise answer to UC §2.2's Astra counterexample (`(n mod 2, 0)`: the homomorphism is sufficient, not necessary): the graph up to observational congruence on the summary side is as weak as the semantic relation, because up to bisimilarity is sound (Definition 6.2.2), while the graph up to *equality* is the homomorphism and is too strong.

*Which smaller relations suffice: TRACE's differential, and COH's diamonds.* TRACE compares every field each turn (`fn2-representation-relation`), the largest useful relation and the cheapest to state; nothing smaller is needed while both sides allocate identically. The place where TRACE-style comparison needs an up-to is COH §5.1's G2 diamond checks: two orders of two admissions put the two connections in different rows, pool slots and op rows, so the endpoints differ by a bijection of identifiers and are not equal. The endpoint relation `≈` for such diamonds is **equality up to a consistent renaming** of `(kind, slot)` rows, pool slots and op rows, with their generations carried along. ## 7. Descent and the cocycle condition

**Works.** Vistoli, "Notes on Grothendieck topologies, fibered categories and descent theory", 2007 (`vistoli2004-grothendieck-topologies-fibered-categories-descent`, arXiv math/0412512 v4; printed pages; ch. 4 read); Abramsky and Brandenburger, "The sheaf-theoretic structure of non-locality and contextuality", NJP 2011 (`abramsky-brandenburger2011-sheaf-theoretic-contextuality`, in paperbin; PDF pages of the arXiv version). UC §5.2 already read Abramsky-Brandenburger and cut the sheaf reading to one prediction ("no node can certify the global absence of a conflict from its local data"); Astra's item 9 cut the witness "sheaf condition". This entry asks what *descent* adds that a sheaf does not: gluing along recorded isomorphisms, with a condition on triples.

**(a) What they say.**
- Vistoli, Definition 4.2 (p. 71): "An object with descent data ({ξᵢ}, {φᵢⱼ}) on U, is a collection of objects ξᵢ ∈ F(Uᵢ), together with isomorphisms φᵢⱼ : pr₂*ξⱼ ≃ pr₁*ξᵢ in F(Uᵢ ×_U Uⱼ), such that the following cocycle condition is satisfied. For any triple of indices i, j and k, we have the equality pr₁₃*φᵢₖ = pr₁₂*φᵢⱼ ∘ pr₂₃*φⱼₖ ... The isomorphisms φᵢⱼ are called transition isomorphisms." Definition 4.8 (p. 76): descent data are "effective if [they are] isomorphic to the image of an object of F(U)", and "F is a stack if and only if it is a prestack, and all objects with descent data in F are effective." The proof of Proposition 4.1 (p. 70) shows what the cocycle buys: the relation "xᵢ = φᵢⱼ xⱼ" on the disjoint union is reflexive because φᵢᵢ = φᵢᵢ ∘ φᵢᵢ, symmetric because φᵢᵢ = φᵢⱼ ∘ φⱼᵢ, "and transitivity follows directly from the general cocycle condition", so the quotient is the glued object.
- Abramsky-Brandenburger §2.5 (p. 7): an empirical model is "a compatible family for the cover M ... for all C, C′ ∈ M, e_C|C ∩ C′ = e_C′|C ∩ C′"; §3 (p. 10): "the entire issue of non-locality and contextuality ... is equivalently formulated as the non-existence of global sections for the corresponding compatible families." The abstract: maximal contextuality is "characterized in purely qualitative terms, as the non-existence of global sections in the support" (the possibilistic case, Hardy and GHZ, which needs no probabilities).

**(b) What fn2 does because of it.**

*Peer convergence as descent, stated.* Take the peers `1..n` of a federation as the cover, each peer's served view of a group (its Binding of validated identity facts, UC §4.3, plus its local numbering and served-content choices) as `ξᵢ`, the overlap `Uᵢⱼ` as what both peers hold, and a transition map `φᵢⱼ` as the identification of `j`'s copy of an overlapping item with `i`'s. Then **the federation theorem is effectiveness of descent data**: the peers' views glue into one global view, unique up to isomorphism, iff every `φᵢⱼ` is an isomorphism on the overlap and, for every triple, `φᵢₖ = φᵢⱼ ∘ φⱼₖ` on `Uᵢⱼₖ`. Two nodes never test it (the cocycle is vacuous for a pair); three do, and for a federation in which every pair overlaps, the triangles are a finite basis: checking all `C(n, 3)` triples suffices for every loop of identifications, because every loop in the filled complete complex reduces to triangles. That is COH's H2 ("Coherent route data ... agree along routes declared interchangeable", COHERENCE.md:289) at the federation level, with a finite generating set where COH has none.

*What record kind carries the transition maps, and why fn2 should keep it trivial.* If every cross-node identification is *derived from a content key* (a Message-ID with its content Name, the `(content identity, binding evidence)` fact of UC §4.3), then `φᵢⱼ = κᵢ ∘ κⱼ⁻¹` for the per-node key maps `κ`, and the cocycle holds identically: `κᵢκⱼ⁻¹κⱼκₖ⁻¹ = κᵢκₖ⁻¹`. In descent language every such transition cocycle is a coboundary, so its class is trivial and there is nothing to check. Local article numbers are exactly this case (each node's numbering is its own `κ`), which is why UC §4.3 can say article numbers do not converge and nothing breaks. The cocycle becomes a real check only when a pairwise correspondence is *recorded as data*, not derived: a catch-up alignment "your position p is my position q", a transported domain's position map `ρ` (MK R9, TR §2.4, COH Q11) composed across two moves, or a consumer cursor carried from node A to node B. Instructions:
1. **Every cross-node correspondence is derived from a content key, never stored as a numeric pair**, in the peering design (E4, RELEASE-PLAN §3 contract item 4) and in the consumer's failover across nodes (CLIENTS §5 Q2). A cursor that moves between nodes moves as a set of content keys at a cut (or the last delivered key), and the target derives its own position. Then the federation's descent data are coboundaries and the cocycle needs no checker.
2. **Where a pairwise map must be recorded (a transported domain's `ρ`), it is a record kind with its endpoints, and the triangle is checked.** MK's `migration`/`port` records already carry position maps for transport (MK §0 table, M2 record kinds); add to TR Experiment D's acceptance (TRANSPORT.md:352-374) the federation form: for three nodes and two transports, `ρ_{n→n''}` recorded directly agrees with `ρ_{n'→n''} ∘ ρ_{n→n'}` on every position both define. A failure names the triple and the first position where they differ.

*What fails when peers cannot be glued, and what fn2 reports.* Two different things, and the distinction is the use of Abramsky-Brandenburger here.
- **A key-derived federation always glues as a set of facts**: union of validated facts is a join, and a family of sub-sets that agree on overlaps has its union as global section. What can fail is that the glued Binding is not a *function* (two contents for one Message-ID). fn2 already reports that: `conflicted` (UC §4.3), recorded as evidence under an admission bound. No cohomology class is needed to name it.
- **A constraint of arity three or more can be satisfied by every pair of peers' views and by no global view.** This is Abramsky-Brandenburger's possibilistic contextuality in fn2's terms, and its smallest instance is the ledger of UC §9.3: an account with balance 2 and three peers each accepting one transfer of 1; every pair of acceptances is consistent, the three are not. Binary conflicts ("at most one content per Message-ID", "at most one holder per claim") are pairwise by definition, so pairwise-compatible views always glue for them; "at most k of these" for k ≥ 2 over facts accepted at different peers is not. Instruction for UC §5.1's per-operation properties (UNIVERSAL-CONSTRUCTIONS.md:205-226) and for every Binding-like mergeable kind: **each declared cross-peer constraint states its arity.** Upward-closed predicates (k-of-G witnesses, `conflicted`) and binary exclusions may be merged by union with conflicts recorded; an exclusion of arity ≥ 3 is not a mergeable fact and is decided in one domain by Decide, which UC §5.1 already requires for `balance ≥ 0` and which this rule makes a declaration-time check instead of a case-by-case argument. A three-peer simulator scenario (three peers, a budget of 2, three offers, each pair delivered first in turn) is its tooth: a design that merges such a constraint accepts all three somewhere.

**(c) Cost.** Item 1: a design rule in E4's and E3's specifications, no code until peering is built. Item 2: one acceptance clause in Experiment D (deferred with D). The arity declaration: one field on mergeable kinds when layer D/E declares them, and one scenario.

**(d) Verdict: NAME ONLY** for the federation theorem as descent (with key-derived maps it is a coboundary and holds by construction); **USE AT E** for the two rules (key-derived cross-node correspondences; the arity of cross-peer constraints) and the three-peer scenario; **USE AT Experiment D** for the triangle check on recorded `ρ`. Nothing for a cohomology computation: in fn2's design the only obstructions are a non-functional Binding (already `conflicted`) and a non-binary constraint (decided, not merged).

## 8. Beck-Chevalley and fibrations

**Works.** Seely, "Hyperdoctrines, natural deduction and the Beck condition", Z. Math. Logik Grundlag. Math. 29, 1983 (`seely1983-hyperdoctrines-natural-deduction-beck-condition`, printed pp. 505-542); Jacobs, *Categorical Logic and Type Theory*, Elsevier 1999 (`jacobs1999-categorical-logic-and-type-theory`, printed pages; ch. 1 §§1.8-1.9 and ch. 4 read for quantification and subset types). Already in paperbin and used for the data-migration reading: `spivak2010-functorial-data-migration`, `johnson-rosebrugh-wood2012-lenses-fibrations-universal-translations`.

**(a) What they say.**
- Seely, introduction (p. 505): "in a given hyperdoctrine, the Beck condition for a pullback diagram is just the condition that the corresponding theory 'recognizes' the pullback." The condition itself (p. 511): "(5′)(i) for each t : X → Y in T, t* has adjoints Σ_t ⊣ t* ⊣ Π_t, and (ii) if [the square of t, t′] is a pullback in T, and φ ∈ |P(Y)|, then the morphism Σ_t′ t″*φ → t*Σ_t φ is an isomorphism"; "condition (5′)(ii) is called Beck condition. Note that the analogous condition for Π must hold."
- Jacobs, Definition 1.9.4 (p. 97): a fibration "has products (resp. coproducts) if ... every substitution functor u* has a right adjoint Π_u (resp. a left adjoint Σ_u); the Beck-Chevalley condition holds: for every pullback in B ... the canonical natural transformation ... is an isomorphism." Its meaning (p. 96): "the Beck-Chevalley condition guarantees that the induced structure is preserved by reindexing functors (and hence that it is essentially the same in all fibres). This Beck-Chevalley condition is not a formality: it may fail, see Exercise 1.9.10." Exercise 1.9.10 (p. 106) is Pitts' counterexample: for Scott-closed subsets the left adjoint along a projection is "the Scott-closure of {x ∈ X | ∃y ∈ Y. (x, y) ∈ A}", and Beck-Chevalley "would hold" only if the image "is already Scott-closed", which fails for `X = N ∪ {∞}`: the quantifier exists and is not stable under substitution, because a closure is applied after the image.

**(b) What fn2 does because of it.**

*Views as a fibration over principals, and the square to state.* With MK's grant-first authorization (MK §5.2; decision 3) and UC §2.5's projections as functorial data migration, a principal's view of a fact set is reindexing along the principal's grant, `g* : Facts → Facts_g` (restriction to what the grant covers), and a projection or migration of the facts is a quantifier along a schema map `m`: `Σ_m` for an image, a count or an aggregate (UC's Σ), `Π_m` for a universal read (UC §3.2). Beck-Chevalley for a migration and a view is the isomorphism

`g′* Σ_m  ≅  Σ_{m′} g*`  (and the same for `Π`)

for the pullback of `m` along `g`: **projecting and then restricting to what the principal may see is the same as restricting and then projecting.** In sets with plain subset reindexing it holds (the subobject fibration of Set is regular), which is why the theory says something only where fn2's views are *not* plain reindexing. Pitts' example names the two ways they stop being plain:
1. **An aggregate computed before the restriction.** A count, a low/high water mark, a "has unread", a size: `Σ_m` taken over all facts, then shown to a principal whose grant hides some of them. Then `g′*Σ_m ⊋ Σ_{m′}g*` in what it reveals: the count includes hidden articles. That is the **leak**.
2. **A closure after the image.** "You may see a thread if you may see any message in it", "a group is listed if any article is readable", or Pitts' Scott-closure: the view is closed under a rule after the image is taken, so a restriction applied first and a restriction applied after differ. Depending on the direction of the rule, that is a leak or a **hide** (a principal loses an item that restriction-first would have shown).

*The defect it detects, and the check.* For every reply or projection that carries an aggregate or a closure, the two routes must agree on a fixture where the grant hides something, or the reply must declare the aggregate as part of what the grant discloses. Instruction for layer D's WIRE fixtures and E's access rules (RELEASE-PLAN §3 E contract item 2 and the web row "a group the login cannot read is 'no group by that name'"): a **Beck-Chevalley differential** scenario family. For each aggregate-bearing surface (`GROUP`'s `211 count low high`, `LIST ACTIVE`'s water marks, XOVER's range, the consumer's `status` counts, the web reader's per-group counts), run the principal's request against (i) the node, which computes the aggregate however it does, and (ii) the aggregate recomputed from the principal's restricted fact set; they must be equal, or the surface lists the aggregate in its grant's disclosure. The fixture needs one hidden article inside a visible group (an access rule narrower than a group, a removed article, or a signed-only article). Where visibility is whole groups only, the pullback is along a coproduct inclusion and the square holds by construction, so the family is empty until the first per-article rule exists; that is itself the useful statement for E2's design: **per-article visibility makes every group aggregate a Beck-Chevalley obligation**, and E2 should choose it knowing that.

For a schema change (after D68 is revisited, TR §3.2), the same square with `m` the migration: a migration that merges rows (`Σ` along a non-injective map) followed by a grant written against the old schema leaks the merged row to any principal that could see one of its sources, and a migration that drops a field the grant's caveat reads hides. TR §3.2's "Decide compatibility relation checked over obligation kinds" gains the clause: every grant's caveat is re-checked as a pullback along the migration (`Δ_m` of the old grant equals the new grant on migrated facts). Nothing to build before then.

**(c) Cost.** The scenario family: one fixture world with a hidden article and one comparison per aggregate-bearing surface, about a day inside E2 once the access rules exist; zero before per-article visibility. The migration clause: one sentence in TR §3.2 now, nothing built.

**(d) Verdict: USE AT E** (E2's access rules, as the Beck-Chevalley differential and as a design input on per-article visibility); **NAME ONLY** for the fibration of views (MK's pullback visibility is reindexing; the name says what must commute with it).

## 9. Cost enrichment and provenance semirings

**Works.** Lawvere, "Metric spaces, generalized logic, and closed categories", Rend. Sem. Mat. Fis. Milano 43, 1973, reprinted TAC Reprints 1, 2002 (`lawvere1973-metric-spaces-generalized-logic-closed-categories`, scan OCR; original pagination 135-166, Scry `academic:10.1007/bf02924844`); Green, Karvounarakis and Tannen, "Provenance semirings", PODS 2007 (`green-karvounarakis-tannen-provenance-semirings-pods2007`, in paperbin; section numbers, the preprint has no stable pages). The Grädel-Tannen semiring papers beside it in paperbin were not needed.

**(a) What they say.**
- Lawvere, 2002 commentary (p. 1): "The closed interval [0,∞] of real numbers as objects, ≥ as maps, + as 'tensor' and truncated subtraction as adjoint 'hom', constitute a bona fide example of a complete, symmetric, monoidal closed category V." The analogy that starts the paper (Sunto, p. 135): "dist(a, b) + dist(b, c) ≥ dist(a, c) e hom(A, B) ⊗ hom(B, C) → hom(A, C)": the triangle inequality is composition. In the closed category of quantities, "Hom(a, c) = c − a if c ≥ a, 0 if a ≥ c, so that the Hom-⊗ adjointness reduces (if we denote by 'minus' this truncated subtraction) to the if-and-only-if [b + a ≥ c iff b ≥ c ∸ a]" (pp. 140-141; the displayed formula is lost in the scan and restored from the adjunction it names), while "in the closed category 2 ... it reduces essentially to modus ponens and the 'deduction theorem'".
- Green-Karvounarakis-Tannen, Proposition 3.5: "Let h : K → K′ and assume that K, K′ are commutative semirings. The transformation given by h from K-relations to K′-relations commutes with any RA⁺ query, q(h(R)) = h(q(R)), if and only if h is a semiring homomorphism." Why-provenance "turns out to be exactly Definition 3.2 for the semiring (P(X), ∪, ∪, ∅, ∅)" and is too coarse: "(f, e) and (d, e) have the same why-provenance ... However, the query can also calculate (f, e) from s alone and (d, e) from r alone" (§4). Proposition 4.2: for any valuation `v : X → K` "there exists a unique homomorphism of semirings Eval_v : N[X] → K"; Theorem 4.3: "For any RA⁺ query q we have q(R) = Eval_v ∘ q(R̄)": every annotated evaluation factors through the provenance polynomials.

**(b) What fn2 does because of it.**

*Metering as enrichment: is the per-turn bound a theorem of composition?* Yes, and it should be proved as one. Read the executor's work as a category enriched in `([0, ∞], ≥, +)`: an object is a core state, the cost of a transition is a quantity, and the composite's cost is the sum, bounded above by the sum of bounds (Lawvere's triangle inequality as composition). The owed bound in `fn2-turn-refinement` and `fn2-turn-bounds` (T3; `reference.lisp:234-236`: `visits + turn-bytes + turn-steps + 1 ≤ 24·H + 4129·K + 1`) is exactly that: a turn is a word of at most `H` observation transitions and `K` visits, each charged at most its declared maximum (`4129 = 4096` bytes `+ 32` steps `+ 1` visit per run, 24 per observation), and the bound is the sum of the per-letter maxima. Two instructions for the proof route (RELEASE-PLAN §4, T3 is Route 2/3 work):
1. **Prove T3 by instantiating one composition lemma, not by induction over the concrete loop.** A generic book states: for a word of transitions each of whose costs is at most `c(kind)`, the word's cost is at most `Σ count(kind)·c(kind)`. R7's generated per-arm cost certificates (`lanes/a-quests/R7.prompt` item 1(b), "a per-arm cost function ... the scalar-work certificate of §B.3") supply `c(kind)`; T3 is the instance with `count(observation) ≤ H`, `count(visit) ≤ K`. The same lemma, instantiated per principal over an operation's transitions across turns, is ACCOUNT's per-operation charge (A6), so the two are one argument.
2. **The residual budget is the internal hom, and its law is one lemma.** The reference passes `(- a actions)` to the next transition (`reference.lisp:135`) and every arm is charged against what remains. Lawvere's adjunction `spent + c ≤ A ⇔ c ≤ A ∸ spent` is the statement that a continuation handed the residual cannot overspend the turn; with truncated subtraction it also covers the exhausted case (`A ∸ spent = 0` admits only zero-cost continuations, the "budget exhaustion leaves a resumable continuation" of EXEC rule 3). ## 10. Markov categories

**Works.** Fritz, "A synthetic approach to Markov kernels, conditional independence and theorems on sufficient statistics", Advances in Mathematics 370, 2020 (`fritz2020-synthetic-markov-kernels-conditional-independence-sufficient-statistics`, arXiv 1908.07021 v8; PDF pages).

**(a) What it says.**
- Definition 2.1 (p. 10): "A Markov category C is a symmetric monoidal category in which every object X ∈ C is equipped with a commutative comonoid structure given by a comultiplication copy_X : X → X ⊗ X and a counit del_X : X → I ... and naturality of del". "We usually think of a morphism in C as some form of Markov kernel, meaning that it assigns to every input value a probability distribution over output values" (p. 10).
- §6, the Gaussian Markov category (pp. 25-26): morphisms `n → m` are triples `(M, C, s)`, "Y := MX + ξ, where ξ is Gaussian noise independent of X with mean E[ξ] := s and covariance matrix ... C", and "(N, D, t) ∘ (M, C, s) := (NM, NCNᵀ + D, Ns + t)"; "forgetting the covariance matrix of every morphism, (M, C, s) ↦ (M, s), implements a strong monoidal functor from Gauss to the category of affine maps". Proposition 6.1: "There is a faithful Markov functor Gauss → Stoch".
- Definition 10.1 (p. 30): "A morphism f : X → Y in a Markov category is deterministic if it respects the comultiplication" (`copy ∘ f = (f ⊗ f) ∘ copy`); Example 10.7 (p. 32): "(M, C, s) is deterministic if and only if C = 0, which indeed means if and only if there is no randomness involved."
- Definition 14.3 (p. 84): "A statistic s : X → V for a statistical model p : Θ → X is sufficient if there is α : V → X such that" the copy diagram of `p` factors through `s` and `α`: the summary loses nothing the model's parameter can affect.

**(b) What fn2 does because of it.**

*Declared loss as a Markov kernel.* MK's D4 (MODAL-KERNEL.md:265) keeps one record kind `declared-loss(source, variant)` with variants `kernel(identified states)`, `unavailable(interval)`, `estimate(method, uncertainty)`, `omitted-fields(list)`, "each variant keeps its own composition rule, and composing two losses keeps both, never a scalar". Fritz supplies the rules by naming the category each variant composes in:
- `kernel(κ)`: a reader of a lossy port or projection learns the source only up to `κ`, which is a total multivalued map (the nonempty-powerset Kleisli category, the possibilistic Markov category). Composition is relational composition, which is MK §5.1's "composition pulls the later kernel back through the earlier reduct" in other words.
- `estimate(method, uncertainty)` for a linear-Gaussian estimator: a morphism of Gauss, composed by `(NM, NCNᵀ + D, Ns + t)`. A Kalman predict step is exactly such a composition; its update (conditioning) exists in Gauss (§11, Example 11.8).
- `unavailable(interval)` and `omitted-fields(list)`: deterministic quotients; composition is union of the intervals or fields.
- Across variants, "keeps both" (MK) is the safe default; when a reader needs one composite, the Markov functors (Gauss → Stoch, and the support map to the possibilistic category) say where it lives: the coarser of the two, and the record names that variant.

*Is "loss travels with every read" a composition law?* Yes: it says the read API is a functor into the Markov category of the variant, not into its deterministic image. Fritz's p. 26 sentence is the failure mode stated as a theorem: dropping the covariance is itself a strong monoidal functor, so a read path that returns means without covariances composes consistently and silently discards what WIDEST-ROLE calls state ("declared loss as state that travels with every read (a covariance is state)", WIDEST-ROLE.md:23). The instruction for D4's implementation, when its first producer lands: the read result type of a lossy fold or port is the variant's morphism (for an estimate, the pair of value and uncertainty), and every composite read composes in that category; a projection that drops the loss component must be a declared `omitted-fields` loss of its own, never an unrecorded forgetful step.

*The determinism law, which is the one fn2 can violate.* Definition 10.1 separates reporting a distribution from sampling one. fn2's folds are deterministic and report parameters (a mean and a covariance are a deterministic value of a distribution), so `copy` commutes with them and two readers of one estimate agree, as replay (COH Q8) requires. The violation would be a lossy projection implemented by sampling at read time, the design INBOX-DELIVERY.md:46 records from Bluesky (`loss_factor` dropping writes at random) and already rejects for inboxes. The rule, for any future lossy timeline or sampled fold: the random choice is made once, by Decide, and recorded (an `omitted-fields`/`kernel` loss with the choice as data), so that every read is a deterministic morphism of the recorded state. That keeps EXEC rule 10's replay intact.

*Kalman fold as a morphism, and summary adequacy.* For a probabilistic fold, UC §2.2's adequacy ("a summary is adequate iff it preserves future observations") is Definition 14.3's sufficiency: the summary `(mean, covariance)` of a linear-Gaussian stream is sufficient, and Theorem 14.5, which Fritz calls "an abstract close relative of the Fisher–Neyman factorization theorem" (p. 4), is the certificate shape. No such fold exists in 5.1.1 (the producers are WIDEST-ROLE's physical-and-offplanet worlds and Wild Earth), so there is nothing to build.

**(c) Cost.** Nothing now. When D4's first producer is built (a lossy NNTP port at E, or a transient live-plane item), each variant's composition rule is a small function and a law test (associativity on three generated losses, and for `estimate` the Gauss formula against a numeric reference), about a day.

**(d) Verdict: NAME ONLY** for declared loss as a Markov kernel and for "loss travels with every read" as functoriality into the variant's category; **USE AT E** for the per-variant composition rules and the rule that sampling is decided and recorded, never done at read time; **NOTHING** for a Kalman fold in 5.1.1.

## 11. Patch theory

**Works.** Angiuli, Morehouse, Licata and Harper, "Homotopical patch theory", ICFP 2014 (`angiuli-morehouse-licata-harper2014-homotopical-patch-theory`, in paperbin; PDF pages of the preprint); Mimram and Di Giusto, "A categorical theory of patches", MFPS 2013 (`mimram-digiusto2013-categorical-theory-of-patches`, in paperbin; PDF pages). TR §1.3 read Angiuli et al. for Experiment D, "as corrected (Astra 4, 14)"; UC §4.3 cited Mimram-Di Giusto for the Binding. This entry reads both for the anchor and the merge, which neither earlier note did.

**(a) What they say.**
- Angiuli et al. (pp. 1-3): "A patch theory is presented by a higher inductive type, where the points of the type are repository contexts, the paths in the type are patches, and the paths between paths are patch laws"; "Patch laws are represented as 2-dimensional paths between paths ... necessary to reason about syntactic transformations on patches, such as an optimizer ... or a merge, which given two divergent edits, should compute two additional patches that reconcile them." Merge comes from pseudocommutation (p. 4: "pc(f, g) = (h, k) ⟹ f · g = h · k", an involution, with reflection and rotation), and it is not canonical: "if we take pseudocommutation to be the identity function then the merge of a span (h, f) is the cospan (!h, !f). That is, the induced merge operation simply undoes both changes" (p. 5). The construction depends on invertibility: "the requirement that patches be invertible fits very nicely into homotopy type theory, where the path structure of types is undirected" (p. 3).
- Mimram-Di Giusto, Definition 1 (p. 3): "A morphism between two files A : [m] → L and B : [n] → L is an injective increasing partial function f : [m] → [n] such that ∀i ∈ [m], B ∘ f(i) = A(i) whenever f(i) is defined. Such a morphism is called a patch." Merge is a pushout (p. 2): residuals "should be computed in the most general way, we formally define them as the arrows of pushout cocones ... However, as expected, not every pair of coinitial morphisms have a pushout in the category L: this reflects the fact that two patches can be conflicting." Definition 5 (p. 5): "The category P is the free finite conservative cocompletion of L", whose objects are "files whose lines are only partially ordered" (p. 5); §6 extends the description to deletions (p. 12).

**(b) What fn2 does because of it.**

*The commons' Anchor with transport is a functor into Mimram-Di Giusto's category.* WIDEST-ROLE's Anchor (WIDEST-ROLE.md:31, "a position region inside a version with a functorial law of motion along edits") and the transclusive commons' rule that "every edit event declares how it transports positions, so the anchor can be carried along the history to any later version, with the result typed as *unchanged, moved, edited, split, deleted* or *withdrawn*" (`widest/transclusive-commons.md:9`) are precisely: each edit event denotes a patch in `L` (an injective increasing partial map of positions, undefined on deleted positions), and transporting an anchor along a history is composing those maps. Functoriality is then by construction, and the typed results are read off the composite: unchanged and moved are defined points, deleted is undefined, split is a span whose image is not contiguous. Instructions, for whichever quest builds the first editing consumer (TR Experiment C's trigger; the commons is the target application):
1. **An edit event kind's transport is its `L`-morphism, declared with the kind and checked as one.** The declaration states the partial map (insertion offsets, deleted ranges); the checker verifies that it is injective and increasing on its domain and that it agrees with the edit's effect on content (`B ∘ f(i) = A(i)`, Definition 1's condition, which is the anchor's guarantee that it still points at the same element). Anchor transport is then composition, never a per-anchor replay of edits.
2. **Within one commit domain no pushout is needed.** UC §5.4 item 2 already chooses server reconciliation: Decide orders the edits, and a client's rebase of its pending edit over the server's intervening ones is the pushout leg `h/g` where the pushout exists. Where it does not (two edits of the same span), Decide's order *chooses* a cocone instead of a universal one, and that choice is the outcome the client is told. So the in-domain case uses `L` for transport and Decide for conflicts, which is what UC decided, now with the category named.
3. **Across domains (a fork promoted into its parent, two peers' replicas of one document), a merge-capable fold declares which of two merges it is**: the pushout in `L` with conflicts refused to Decide in the receiving domain, or the pushout in `P` with conflicts kept as values (lines only partially ordered, Pijul's representation). The second is the document analogue of the Binding, which keeps conflicting contents as a validated multivalue (UC §4.3), and the reason UC could cite Mimram-Di Giusto there: the Binding is the free completion of single-valued assignment under pushouts. A fold that declares neither has no merge, and promotion of a fork that edited it is refused by name.

*What the 2-cell "two migration routes agree" is.* There are two kinds, and COH already separates them: thin 2-cells (equal observations, COH Q10 "for observations") and data-carrying ones (equal position maps, converted cuts, losses). Angiuli et al.'s patch laws are the thin kind in a groupoid: they presuppose invertible patches, and fn2's migrations are not invertible (lossy and `ext` grades, MK M2; TR's lossy `w`), so the homotopical setting does not fit beyond the name. Mimram-Di Giusto's `L` is the category the data-carrying part lives in: a route's position map is an `L`-morphism (partial, injective, increasing), and two routes agree on positions iff their composites are the *same* partial map.

*Is this Experiment D's formal content?* Half of it. D (TRANSPORT.md:352-374) checks declared 2-cells on observations over all future suffixes, the thin half. It has no merges (a route graph is not a span with a pushout) and does not compare position maps. Instruction for D's acceptance, when its trigger arrives: for each declared 2-cell, also compare the two routes' position maps as `L`-morphisms on every journal it enumerates (defined at the same positions, equal where defined), and report a disagreement as a data-carrying 2-cell failure distinct from an observation failure. With that clause D is the formal content of route coherence for migrations; merges stay with items 2-3.

**(c) Cost.** Item 1: a declaration field per edit kind and a checker of three conditions, a day, at the first editing consumer. Items 2-3: a declaration on merge-capable folds and one refusal word, at the same time. The Experiment D clause: an extra comparison in D's enumerator, an hour, when D runs.

**(d) Verdict: USE AT the first editing consumer** (anchors as `L`-morphisms; the declared merge kind); **USE AT Experiment D** (position-map equality as the data-carrying 2-cell); Angiuli et al.'s homotopical reading: **NAME ONLY** (it names the thin 2-cells and does not fit non-invertible migrations).

## 12. Heterogeneous specification

**Works.** Mossakowski, Maeder and Lüttich, "The heterogeneous tool set, Hets", TACAS 2007 (`mossakowski-maeder-luttich2007-heterogeneous-tool-set-hets`, the CEUR VERIFY 2007 text, which carries the LNCS pagination 119-135 in its headers); Goguen and Burstall, "Institutions: abstract model theory for specification and programming", JACM 1992 (`goguen-burstall1992-institutions-abstract-model-theory`, in paperbin; MK uses its satisfaction condition) as the stand-in for Diaconescu, whose book is not in paperbin and is not needed for what follows. MK §4.2-4.3 already treats a port as a theory morphism with a reduct and composition of worlds as a pushout, and MK §4.6 says what Specware would compute; this entry adds only what Hets has that Specware does not: comorphisms between *different* logics, and development graphs with conservativity and decomposition of proof obligations.

**(a) What they say.**
- The satisfaction condition (p. 121): "truth is invariant under change of notation ... M′ ⊨_Σ′ σ(φ) ⇔ M′|σ ⊨_Σ φ", and for a comorphism between institutions I and J (p. 122): "M′ ⊨^J_Φ(Σ) α_Σ(φ) ⇔ β_Σ(M′) ⊨^I_Σ φ. Here, Φ(Σ) is the translation of signature Σ from institution I to institution J, α_Σ(φ) is the translation of the Σ-sentence φ ... and β_Σ(M′) is the translation (or perhaps: reduction) of the Φ(Σ)-model M′ to a Σ-model."
- The Grothendieck institution (p. 122): "basically a flattening, or disjoint union, of the logic graph ... there is no direct interaction of e.g. logical connectives from different logics that lead to new sentences ... they interact through the comorphisms."
- Development graphs (pp. 127-128): nodes with signatures and local axioms, "definition links, indicating the dependency of each involved structured specification on its subparts", and "theorem links ... the central data structure to represent proof obligations ... a global theorem link postulates that all axioms of the source node (including the inherited ones) hold in the target node"; "The proof calculus for development graphs ... allow[s] for proving global theorem links by decomposing them into simpler (local and global) ones". And (p. 124) "a conservativity checker can check whether a theory extension is conservative (i.e. does not lead to new theorems)."

**(b) What fn2 does because of it.**

*Ports as comorphisms: where fn2 really is heterogeneous.* Inside the kernel, MK is right that a port is a theory morphism (one institution, B's). The heterogeneity is at the wire: a protocol's fixtures are statements about octet transcripts (the wire logic: signatures are a grammar's productions, models are byte-stream machines, sentences are transcript assertions such as "after `GROUP misc.test` the reply is `211 n lo hi misc.test`"), and the kernel's statements are about journals, folds and outcomes. A `defgrammar` port is then a comorphism `(Φ, α, β)` from the wire logic into the kernel's: `Φ` maps productions to kernel commands and outcomes, `α` translates a transcript assertion into an assertion about the outcome, and `β` is the renderer that turns a kernel model's outcome into bytes. The satisfaction condition says: **a kernel outcome satisfies the translated assertion iff its rendered transcript satisfies the original one.** That is the obligation layer D's grammar list does not yet have. D2's five stubs (`build/coordinator/briefs/FN2-LAYER-D-MACHINES-NNTP.md` item 2: totality over arbitrary octets, linear consumption, fragmentation invariance, bounded retained input, reply typing) say the parser and renderer are well behaved; none says the renderer preserves the meaning the fixture asserts. Instruction for D2: add the comorphism condition as a sixth stub per reply production, checked as the WIRE fixtures' *two-level* comparison: each independently authored fixture states its assertion at the wire level and, through `α`, at the outcome level; WIRE checks both on every run. A renderer that writes a wrong count with a correctly typed reply passes "reply typing" and fails the condition. It is also the precise form of RELEASE-PLAN §2.4's warning that "a parser and generator from one declaration can agree on the same wrong protocol": the condition ties the declaration to an oracle at the other level.

*What Hets would compute for the NNTP, consumer and peering tables, and their colimit.* Taking each protocol's table as a signature over the kernel base B (NNTP's 31 keywords, `books/nntp-help.lisp:52-59`; the consumer's 11 request codes, RELEASE-PLAN §1's `fnct.consumer.request` row; the five peering modes), Hets would build a development graph with B at the root, one node per port importing B through its comorphism, and the node's theory as the colimit of the three over B, then compute: (i) the colimit signature, in which only symbols the ports reach through B are identified and every protocol-local symbol stays distinct; (ii) one global theorem link per port ("the node satisfies the port's axioms"), decomposed into local links per production; (iii) for each port, whether the colimit is a conservative extension of it. What fn2 should take from that, as three checks on data the DSLs already have or will have after §1 and §2:
1. **The amalgamation table.** For every base symbol (a stream kind, the identity Binding, article numbering per group, a consumer's cursor, a principal's account), the ports that read it and the ports that write it, generated from each port's declared `:uses`/`:writes` (§1 item 1) and its grammar's productions. A symbol written by two ports is accepted only when its kind is union-merged (the Binding: NNTP POST, TAKETHIS, IHAVE and peering feeds all write identity facts, which is legal by UC §4.3); a Decide-owned symbol with two writers is refused at registration as `stream-owner-conflict`, which is MK §4.3's wiring check applied to every symbol the colimit identifies rather than only to declared boundary streams. Article numbering is the case to watch: NNTP assigns it at commit, and if peering's catch-up ever writes it, the table catches the second writer before any run.
2. **Sorts stay apart in the colimit.** PLANES L5 ("positions, live sequences, ticks, time-domain readings and revisions are five types", MODAL-KERNEL.md notation) is a statement about the colimit signature: an NNTP article number and a consumer's commit position are different sorts that must not be identified by any port (INBOX-DELIVERY.md:207 relates them by monotonicity, not identity). The amalgamation table reports any port that maps a local number sort to a base position sort.
3. **Conservativity as fixture invariance.** MK §2.8's conservativity "extension by extension" has an executable test that needs no prover: the NNTP fixtures' transcripts are byte-identical with the consumer port and the peering port registered and unregistered. It is a bounded check of exactly the property Hets' conservativity checker decides symbolically, and it is the release question E faces when the remote consumer wire lands on the NNTP connection (RELEASE-PLAN §3 E contract item 3: "a short NNTP command followed by an explicitly framed, length-bounded body"): that port must not change a single NNTP transcript.

**(c) Cost.** The sixth grammar stub: part of D2's fixture design, about a day across the NNTP reply productions. The amalgamation table: a registry query over declarations §1 and §2 already add, half a day at D1/E. The fixture-invariance test: one driver flag and a rerun of the NNTP fixtures, an hour, at E3.

**(d) Verdict: USE AT D** for the comorphism condition (D2's sixth stub and WIRE's two-level fixtures) and for the amalgamation table; **USE AT E** for conservativity as fixture invariance; the Grothendieck institution and the development graph themselves: **NAME ONLY** (MK §4 and §2.8 already organise the same structure; Hets the tool: **NOTHING**, since fn2's obligations are discharged by ACL2 and the simulator, and MK §4.6's reason for not building a general institution library stands).
